Measuring Engineering at Scale: A Practical Framework for 30 Teams

📅 January 2026 ⏱️ 12 min read 🏷️ Engineering Leadership, Metrics, DevOps

After leading engineering organizations from 5 to 30 teams, I've learned that what you measure determines what you improve. But measuring the wrong things—or too many things—can be worse than measuring nothing at all.

The Challenge: Two Delivery Models, One Organization

Our engineering organization serves two distinct customer segments with fundamentally different needs:

🏢 Enterprise Model

  • 20 scrum teams
  • Bi-annual release cycles
  • Major contract commitments
  • Extensive coordination requirements
  • Stability over speed

🚀 SMB Model

  • 10 scrum teams
  • Continuous deployment
  • Rapid iteration and feedback
  • Independent team autonomy
  • Speed and innovation focus

Traditional metrics frameworks like DORA or SPACE are excellent starting points, but they don't address this duality. We needed a framework that could measure both models fairly while providing actionable insights.

The Framework: Five Dimensions of Engineering Excellence

After iterating for two years, we landed on five core dimensions. Each dimension is measured consistently but interpreted contextually based on the delivery model.

1. Reliability: Are We Keeping the Lights On?

Reliability is non-negotiable for both models, but the bar is different:

Metric Enterprise Target SMB Target
Uptime 99.9% (contractual SLAs) 99.5% (balance speed vs. stability)
SLA Attainment 100% (zero tolerance) 95% (rapid recovery preferred)
Telemetry Coverage Full observability before deploy Instrument iteratively

💡 Key Insight: Context Matters

Early on, we tried to hold both models to 99.9% uptime. This created friction—SMB teams felt hamstrung by overly cautious processes. By acknowledging different risk profiles, we empowered teams while maintaining accountability.

2. Velocity: Are We Delivering Predictably?

Velocity isn't about moving fast; it's about moving predictably. For enterprise teams, this means hitting release commitments. For SMB teams, it's maintaining consistent throughput.

  • Team Velocity Trends: Are teams maintaining consistent output, or are we seeing decline? We track story points and work items completed per sprint, looking for 10% variation or less.
  • Deployment Frequency: SMB teams deploy multiple times per day; enterprise teams deploy on schedule. Both are "good" if they're predictable.
  • Release Schedule Integrity: For enterprise, this is critical—did we deliver what we committed? Target: 90%+ features delivered per release.

⚠️ Common Pitfall: Comparing Apples to Oranges

We used to rank teams by velocity. This created perverse incentives—teams inflated story points or cherry-picked work. Now we track trends within each team and celebrate improvement, not absolute numbers.

3. Quality: Are We Building It Right?

Quality metrics are where things get interesting. High deployment frequency (SMB) naturally leads to higher absolute defect counts, but that doesn't mean lower quality.

  • Change Failure Rate: What percentage of deployments cause production issues? Target <15% for both models. SMB teams often do better here due to smaller, incremental changes.
  • Customer Defect Trends: Track incoming vs. fixed defects by severity. The goal isn't zero defects—it's a healthy flow where high-severity issues are fixed quickly.
  • Maintenance SLO Compliance: Are we hitting our internal commitments for defect resolution? High severity: 48 hours. Medium: 2 weeks. Low: backlog prioritization.

The breakthrough came when we separated defect discovery rate from defect resolution speed. High deployment frequency means you find issues faster—that's a good thing if you fix them quickly.

4. Security: Are We Protecting Our Customers?

Security is a shared responsibility, measured consistently across both models:

  • Secure Score: A composite metric (0-100) covering code scanning, dependency vulnerabilities, access controls, and security training compliance. Target: 85+ for all teams.
  • SOC 2 State: For enterprise especially, SOC 2 compliance is table stakes. We track open findings by severity and maintain zero critical/high severity findings.

Security metrics are "gating"—if your score drops below threshold, feature work pauses until it's addressed. This has teeth because leadership enforces it.

5. Focus: Are We Working on the Right Things?

This dimension emerged from a painful realization: teams were hitting their metrics but still felt underwater. Why? Because 80% of their time was spent on maintenance and tech debt.

  • Work Breakdown Analysis: What percentage of engineering time goes to maintenance vs. tech debt vs. innovation? We target 40% maintenance, 30% tech debt, 30% innovation—but acknowledge that enterprise teams often skew more toward maintenance due to the product maturity.
  • Staffing Mix: Are we staffing for the work we have? If 70% of work is maintenance, but 70% of engineers are senior developers who want to innovate, we have a mismatch.

🎯 Real Example: Rebalancing Focus

One quarter, we discovered three teams were spending 65%+ time on maintenance. After analysis, we found a common root cause: an aging shared service. We carved out a dedicated team to modernize it over six months. Within two quarters, those three teams had rebalanced to 40/30/30, and their velocity increased by 25%.

Implementation: From Framework to Action

A framework is worthless if it lives in a slide deck. Here's how we operationalized it:

Weekly: Team-Level Review

Each team tracks their own metrics in their sprint retrospectives. They own their trends and identify improvement actions.

Monthly: Product-Level Rollup

Product leadership reviews aggregated metrics across their teams. We look for outliers (both good and bad) and share learnings across teams.

Quarterly: Portfolio Review

Executive team reviews organization-wide trends. This is where we make strategic decisions: Do we need to rebalance focus? Are we investing enough in tech debt? Should we adjust targets based on business priorities?

The Data Stack

We automate what we can:

  • Azure DevOps APIs for velocity and work item tracking
  • Application Insights for uptime and telemetry
  • PagerDuty for SLA tracking
  • GitHub Advanced Security for secure score
  • Manual quarterly surveys for work breakdown (surprisingly, this is the hardest to automate accurately)

We publish dashboards in Power BI, but the real magic happens in the conversations these metrics enable.

What We Got Wrong (and How We Fixed It)

Mistake #1: Too Many Metrics

Version 1 of our framework had 30+ metrics. Teams spent more time reporting than improving. We ruthlessly cut to the 15 metrics that actually drive decisions.

Mistake #2: Treating Metrics as Goals

When we tied metrics directly to performance reviews, teams gamed them. Now metrics are diagnostic tools, not evaluation tools. They inform conversations about team health, not individual performance.

Mistake #3: Ignoring Context

A team's velocity dropped 30% one quarter. The metrics flagged it, and leadership panicked. Turns out they'd onboarded three new engineers and deliberately slowed to invest in training. Context is everything.

Mistake #4: Not Listening to Teams

The first version of "Focus" metrics came from the top down. Teams rejected it because it didn't match their reality. Version 2 was co-created with tech leads, and adoption was night and day.

Outcomes: What Changed?

Two years in, here's what we've seen:

  • Reduced firefighting: Change failure rate dropped from 22% to 11% as teams focused on quality over speed.
  • Better planning: Enterprise release integrity improved from 78% to 93%—fewer surprises, happier customers.
  • Strategic tech debt investment: We now allocate ~$2M annually to targeted tech debt reduction based on Focus metrics.
  • Team autonomy with accountability: Teams have clear guardrails (security, quality) but freedom within them.
  • Shared language: Product, engineering, and executive teams speak the same language when discussing trade-offs.

The Meta-Lesson: Metrics Are a Conversation Starter

The biggest mindset shift was this: metrics don't tell you what to do; they tell you what to discuss.

When change failure rate spikes, that's not a sign that the team is failing—it's a signal to ask: "What changed? What did we learn? How do we adapt?" Metrics create transparency, which builds trust, which enables real improvement.

Your Context Is Different—And That's the Point

This framework works for us because it reflects our reality: dual delivery models, 30 teams, specific customer needs. Your reality is different.

The principles are transferable:

  1. Start with what matters to your business
  2. Measure what you can actually influence
  3. Keep it simple—fewer metrics, deeper insights
  4. Build the framework with your teams, not for them
  5. Treat metrics as conversation starters, not scorecards
