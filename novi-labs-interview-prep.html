Section 7

Likely Q&A — Scripted Answers

"Why are you interested in going from 400 people back to 35?"
Architectural choices at 35 determine what's possible at 150. I've been on the other side of those decisions — I know what happens when they get locked in wrong. The opportunity to make them intentionally, before they're irreversible, is rare. That's more interesting to me than optimizing an org that's already at scale.
"What's your philosophy on AI in a product like ours?"
Before it's a feature problem, it's a trust problem. When your forecast says 'drill here' and an operator commits $8 million based on that number, they need to understand the confidence interval, the key assumptions, and where the model is operating outside its training distribution. Surface the uncertainty, not just the output. That's the difference between a tool that gets used and one that gets blamed after the fact.
"How do you govern AI in production?"
110 post-mortems — each one has a dollar amount, a date, and a root cause. From those we built a deploy checklist that the model cannot skip. The governance has to match the velocity of the team. If it slows people down more than it catches failures, it gets ignored. If it's too loose, you find out in production. Getting that calibration right is the actual work.
"What's your experience with ML and predictive modeling?"
I'm not a data scientist and I won't pretend to be. What I build is the org around trustworthy models — the validation framework, confidence thresholds, human review gates, and post-deployment monitoring. I've learned enough to ask the right questions and to know when a model result should be trusted, challenged, or escalated. Jorge's depth on completions ML is an asset, not a gap I need to fill myself.
"Walk me through your first 90 days."
First 30: Kevin transition. I've been on the other side of this — I replaced the sitting CTO at Quorum, ran the knowledge transfer, and repositioned him as Principal Architect. I know what gets lost and how to capture it. Days 30–60: architecture audit focused on decisions that look reasonable at 35 but become structural problems at 150 — data model, API contracts, multi-tenant partitioning, observability gaps. Days 60–90: roadmap conversation with you and Orion, anchored in what I found.
"What's your view on our competitive position against Enverus?"
Enverus is the incumbent for a reason — breadth, distribution, brand. But the NDN moat isn't something they can buy or build. It requires operator cooperation at a scale that's structurally incompatible with their market position. The right CTO move isn't to add features — it's to make the data network so deep and the switching costs so high that the mid-market can't imagine operating without Novi.
"How would you scale the data pipeline as NDN grows?"
I'd ask where the bottlenecks are hiding before I prescribe anything. Ingestion latency, query performance under multi-tenant load, allocation algorithm accuracy as operator count scales. At Quorum we hit a multi-tenancy wall at around 200 enterprise customers — the data model choices we'd made at 50 customers became load-bearing walls. I'd want to find those walls before we're inside them.
"How do you think about technical debt at this stage?"
The debt that kills you isn't shortcuts — it's structural decisions that become unmovable: the data model, the API contracts, multi-tenant partitioning strategy. Those are the ones you can't refactor around later. I'd rather slow down twice in year one to get those right than spend years two through five paying the interest.
"Have you led teams across the full engineering function — not just development?"
Yes — at Quorum I owned development, platform, hosting/devops, QA, customer support, documentation/knowledge, and overall engineering. The full stack of the function, not just product engineering. I've operated at that scope and I'd expect to again.
"Tell me about your leadership style."
One of my direct reports described me as a "true Multiplier — amplifies the capabilities of those around him, sets high standards, and creates urgency without micromanaging." I think that's accurate. My job is to raise the ceiling for the team, not manage the floor.

The framework I rely on is Christopher Avery's Responsibility Process — it distinguishes coping responses like blame, justification, and shame from actually owning outcomes. I've built it into how I run engineering: when something breaks, we ask what happened and how we prevent it, not who did it. At Quorum, that culture translated to 56% → 75% employee engagement and 7% regrettable attrition. People stay when they feel trusted and when they know problems get fixed instead of buried.

Upstream AI — Talking Points & Q&A

Novi's core product is upstream AI. Scott may go deep here — either to test credibility or because it's top of mind with investors. This section gives you the substance to hold the conversation at a CEO level.

What Upstream AI Actually Does at Novi

Novi's Forecast Engine replaces the industry's default approach — biased type curves and slow manual decline-curve analysis — with ML-driven per-well forecasts at scale. The critical differentiators:

  • Per-well, not per-type-curve: Instead of "wells in this area look like this," Forecast Engine gives individual well-level predictions trained on NDN's proprietary data
  • Uncertainty quantification: Returns confidence intervals, not just point estimates — operators see the range, not just the headline number
  • Scenario scale: Hundreds of development scenarios in the time it takes a reservoir team to run one manually
  • A&D valuations: BD teams entering unfamiliar basins use NDN to build forecast models without years of local data — 38% reduction in EUR forecast error vs. operator-only data
  • Cross-operator intelligence: "What did my competitor do differently on the wells adjacent to mine?" — only answerable with NDN's non-public completion data

The 5 Upstream AI Questions Novi Answers (CEO-Level Talking Points)

Business QuestionNovi's Answer
"Should I drill this well?" EUR confidence interval + IRR at current WTI — with uncertainty range, not a single optimistic number
"What's the optimal development plan for this acreage?" Forecast Engine runs hundreds of spacing/zone/timing scenarios; identifies the plan that maximizes NPV
"How does this acquisition compare to my existing portfolio?" NDN cross-operator type curve benchmarking — even in basins where the operator has no history
"What did my competitor do differently on wells next to mine?" NDN's non-public completion data (stage count, cluster spacing, proppant) — the only place this exists
"Am I forecasting my PDP accurately for my bank or investors?" Auditable, unbiased decline curves at well level — replaces the optimism bias in manually-drawn type curves

The Quorum / Novi Customer Overlap

Quorum's Upstream On Demand suite and Novi Labs serve the same E&P operator base — Devon, Coterra, Continental, Civitas — but at different points in the decision chain. Understanding this overlap is a differentiator in conversation with Scott.

Quorum's Layer (Operations & Accounting)

Land management, production operations, well lifecycle reporting, AFE approval, revenue accounting, JIB, SCADA. The system of record for what happened and what it cost. 500+ upstream operator customers. Decision-ready operational data.

Novi's Layer (Capital Allocation & Analytics)

Forecasting, development planning, acquisition valuation, competitor benchmarking. The system that answers what to do next and what it's worth. These decisions come before the Quorum workflows (AFE gets approved after the forecast says drill) and alongside them (PDP forecasts for reserve reports that accounting owns).

The conversation bridge: "At Quorum, I sat across the table from Devon, Coterra, and Continental. I understand how their VP of Engineering evaluates a software purchase — what questions they ask about data integrity, what their IT security requirements look like, how they think about total cost of ownership. The operators on Novi's customer list aren't new territory for me."

Where the Two Stacks Integrate (Integration Opportunity)

  • AFE ↔ Forecast Engine: Capital approval (Quorum AFE) happens after the well economics case (Novi forecast). A natural integration point — Novi's EUR + IRR feeding directly into the AFE workflow
  • Production Operations ↔ NDN: Quorum's On Demand Production Operations tracks actuals; NDN uses operator-shared actuals as training data. Operators contributing to NDN are already running production tracking — the data exists
  • On Demand Data Hub ↔ Novi Analytics: Quorum's Data Hub APIs are designed for third-party analytics integrations. Novi is exactly the kind of analytics layer it was built to support

TIP Don't pitch this as "Quorum and Novi should integrate." Raise it as evidence that you understand the operator's full workflow and where Novi sits in it. The integration opportunity is a bonus signal, not the main point.

Scripted Q&A — Upstream AI Scenarios

"How familiar are you with the technical challenges of production forecasting at scale?"
Deeply enough to be useful, not so deeply I'd confuse myself with a reservoir engineer. The core challenge I understand well is data quality upstream of the model — lease-to-well allocation errors, missing completion data, inconsistent reporting across operators. NDN's value proposition is largely about solving those data quality problems before the model ever runs. The governance problem — how you surface uncertainty, how you prevent operator over-trust in a point estimate — that's territory I've covered in production AI.
"Operators like Devon have sophisticated internal data science teams. Why would they pay for Novi?"
Two reasons. First, NDN — Devon's internal data is Devon's data. Novi gives them the cross-operator signal they cannot produce themselves, regardless of team size or IT budget. Second, build vs. buy economics. Devon's data science team optimizes for Devon's problems. Novi's team has iterated on forecasting across hundreds of operators. The depth of that iteration is the product. Devon's team would spend years catching up to what Novi already knows about their adjacent operators.
"What's your view on where AI is going in upstream oil and gas?"
The near-term value is in decision support with uncertainty surfaced — not automation. Operators are not ready to have an algorithm decide where to drill without a human reviewing the confidence interval and the assumptions. The companies that win are the ones who make AI an amplifier for the reservoir engineer's judgment, not a replacement for it. Novi's approach — per-well forecasts with uncertainty quantification — is aligned with how sophisticated operators want to use AI right now. The trust infrastructure has to be built before the automation story becomes credible.
"What architectural challenges do you see as Novi scales AI across more basins and operators?"
Three areas I'd want to explore: model drift as basin geology and completion practices evolve, multi-tenant data isolation as NDN grows (an operator contributing data needs absolute confidence it doesn't leak to competitors), and the latency/cost tradeoff as you scale from hundreds to thousands of scenarios per operator. The NDN data sharing model is the most interesting architectural challenge — the trust mechanism that makes operators willing to contribute data is the same mechanism that must guarantee they can't see each other's contributions. Getting that architecture right is load-bearing for the entire business model.
"You spent a long time at Quorum — what specifically about Novi's AI approach made you pay attention?"
The proprietary data layer. At Quorum, we had deep operational data — AFEs, production actuals, land records — but it was operator-siloed. The cross-operator signal was always the gap. NDN is solving the exact problem I could see from the other side of the table. And the approach to uncertainty quantification in Forecast Engine — returning confidence intervals, not just point estimates — shows the team understands that the trust problem is the product problem. That's not a common insight at this company stage.

30-60-90 Day Plan

How to use this in the interview: If Scott asks "What would your first 90 days look like?" you can walk through this verbally — or offer to leave it with him. Either signals that you showed up having already done the thinking, not just the research.

Days 1–30 — Know the people. Earn the trust. Then everything else.

A new CTO who spends month one diagnosing systems before building relationships is optimizing for the wrong thing. People tell you what's actually broken — and follow you into hard decisions — only after they trust you. That trust doesn't come from competence alone. It comes from showing up, listening without an agenda, and treating every person on the team as someone worth knowing, not just a source of information.

  • 1:1s with every engineer in the first 10 days — no agenda, no evaluation. Ask about their work, what they're proud of, what frustrates them. Let them talk. Your job is to understand who they are and what they care about, not to audit the system. The technical picture will emerge. The relationship has to be built on purpose.
  • Learn names. Remember them. At ~35 people, there's no excuse. Know what everyone's working on, what they worked on before Novi, what motivates them. This isn't soft — it's how you get honest information when something goes wrong.
  • Build the peer relationship with Kevin Stambaugh first. He built this team and knows where every body is buried. If he sees you as someone who respects what he built, the transition goes smoothly. If he feels replaced or dismissed, it doesn't — no matter what the org chart says.
  • Establish presence with Jorge (Customer Success) and the product side early. Engineering's reputation is built in the hallways between teams. Be the CTO who's curious about customers, not just code.
  • No major process changes in month one. Earn the right first. A change made before trust lands as an edict. The same change made after trust lands as leadership.
  • Diagnostics follow from relationships, not the other way around. The architecture review, the SDLC map, the SOC 2 scope, the incident history — all of that matters and all of it waits until you've shown the team that you're someone worth working for.

Days 31–60 — Diagnose. Prioritize. Establish the baseline.

By day 30 I have enough to form a point of view. Month two is about translating observations into a shared picture of where we are and what matters most.

  • Present a findings memo to Scott and Jon — technical debt map, team capability gaps, top 3 engineering risks, top 3 leverage points. Not a complaint list — a prioritized view of the system.
  • Establish engineering fundamentals — if they don't already exist formally: deployment checklist, test coverage expectations, incident response runbook, on-call rotation. These are the difference between a team that scales and one that burns out.
  • Assess the ML/AI pipeline specifically — how are models trained, validated, versioned, and monitored in production? Model drift and data quality failures are silent killers in an AI product company. What's the alerting story when NDN data quality degrades?
  • Define engineering metrics — deploy frequency, mean time to recovery, test coverage trend, open critical bugs. Establish a baseline before trying to move it.
  • Build the hiring plan — at ~35 employees, every engineer hire is a 3–5% change to the team. I want to know where the capacity gaps are and what kind of hire fills each one before we're in a reactive mode.
  • Align with Orion (VP Product) on roadmap translation — product commitments need to be grounded in engineering capacity. This relationship is the #1 source of credibility with Scott.

Days 61–90 — Commit. Ship something. Set the standard.

By day 90, Scott should be able to see a difference — not just feel good about the hire.

  • Deliver the 6-month engineering roadmap — with capacity model, risk flags, and what we're explicitly not doing. A roadmap without trade-offs is a wish list.

The Operating Framework: Avery's Responsibility Process

The model I use to hold myself and teams accountable is Christopher Avery's Responsibility Process. The mind's default coping sequence when something goes wrong is: Lay Blame → Justify → Shame → Obligation → Quit — before eventually reaching Responsibility. Most teams get stuck somewhere in the middle and call it a postmortem.

I've embedded this explicitly into the AI projects I've built — 110 documented post-mortems with dollar amounts and dates, a deploy checklist agents cannot skip, and a standing rule: when something breaks, state what happened, fix it, document it.
